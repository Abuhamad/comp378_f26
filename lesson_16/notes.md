# 16 — Empirical vs. Certified Robustness Fundamentals

## Abstract

Evaluating robustness against a single attack creates a false sense of security. Gradient masking hides the true vulnerability of a model by breaking gradient-based attacks, but certified robustness provides a formal guarantee: no adversarial example exists within a radius, regardless of attack strategy. This lesson distinguishes empirical robustness (limited by attack choice) from certified robustness (independent of attack), exposes the most common masking defenses, and shows how adaptive attacks bypass them.

## Objectives

- Define empirical robustness and explain why evaluation bias arises from choosing weak attacks.
- Identify the three gradient masking subtypes: shattered gradients, vanishing gradients, and stochastic gradients.
- Detect gradient masking using random search (non-adaptive) and BPDA (adaptive).
- Articulate how BPDA bypasses non-differentiable preprocessing by approximating it in the backward pass only.
- Describe a historical falsely secure defense and the attack breaking it.
- Articulate why empirical robustness cannot replace certified robustness.

## Content

### Empirical Robustness Limitations

The problem of evaluation bias is not theoretical. Attackers broke many published defenses within months of publication because the initial evaluation was insufficient. Evaluating a defense requires knowing:

1. Which attack algorithm the attacker used (FGSM, PGD, C&W, etc.).
2. How many optimization steps the attack performed (FGSM: 1 step, PGD: 20 or more steps, C&W: 1000+ iterations).
3. What step size or learning rate the attack used.
4. What success criterion the attack optimized for (misclassification, maximum loss, specific incorrect class).
5. Whether the attack had white-box access to model weights or only black-box query access.
6. How many images the researchers tested (evaluating on 10 images is unreliable. 1000+ is standard).
7. Whether the attacker ran the attack to convergence or stopped early.

Without these details, a robustness claim is unverifiable. A researcher could claim *"our defense achieves 85% accuracy under attack"* but provide no way for others to reproduce or audit the result. Researchers may have intentionally or unintentionally evaluated the defense using a weak attack.

Researchers evaluate a defense against a set of attacks. Robustness is only as strong as the strongest attack in the set. If researchers try only weak attacks, a model can appear robust when it is not.

Consider a defense paper published in year N claiming 82% accuracy under PGD-20 attack. The following year, researchers apply PGD-50 with larger step sizes or a different loss function, finding the accuracy drops to 34%. Researchers evaluated the defense against an insufficient attack budget. The model lacks robustness. The evaluation proved weak.

Evaluation bias follows from choosing which attack to run. An adversary unconstrained by the researcher's choice will use a stronger attack. Benchmarks must specify the exact attack suite, step count, and loss function used. A claim of robustness without attack details is hollow.

The distinction between adaptive and non-adaptive adversaries matters critically. A non-adaptive adversary uses a fixed attack designed before seeing the defense. An adaptive adversary designs the attack after studying the defense's architecture and weights. Reported robustness must specify which class of adversary the researcher assumed, or the number is meaningless.

*Why does this distinction matter?* A non-adaptive adversary cannot adjust their attack based on knowledge of the defense mechanism. They apply a generic attack (like standard PGD) to many different models. An adaptive adversary, by contrast, studies the specific defense, learns how its gradient computation works, and designs an attack precisely to defeat it. If a defense claims 70% accuracy against a non-adaptive adversary but only 20% against an adaptive adversary, the gap reveals the defense's weakness. The 50 percentage point gap is not a measurement error. It is evidence of a defense relying on non-standard operations or numerical quirks rather than genuine robustness.

Evaluation protocols must also specify the threat model. *Is the attacker white-box (full access to model weights and architecture) or black-box (only query access)?*, *Is the attack query-limited (at most 100 API calls) or unlimited?* A defense robust against black-box attacks may fail instantly against white-box attacks, and vice versa. A black-box attacker cannot compute gradients. They must rely on random search or surrogate models. A white-box attacker computes exact gradients and can design arbitrarily strong adaptive attacks. Reporting robustness without threat-model details creates confusion. A defense claiming 85% accuracy must specify: *against white-box or black-box attacks?* *With what attack budget?* *Against how many attack algorithms?*

Carlini's evaluation guidelines, published in 2019, formalized best practices for reporting robustness: always test against adaptive attacks, always test against multiple attack algorithms (not PGD alone), always report which threat model the adversary assumes, and always provide code so others can verify independently. A defense without reproducible code is unverifiable. A single number (e.g., *"85% accuracy under attack*"*) without these details conveys almost no information.

The guidelines also recommend using standardized attack implementations (like Foolbox or the RobustBench library) to ensure consistency. Researchers should evaluate a defense claiming robustness against reference implementations, not custom attack code of inferior quality. Standardization prevents researchers from unknowingly using weak attacks. Finally, the guidelines recommend: if a defense claims robustness to radius epsilon, evaluate all points within a ball of radius epsilon, not only points at the boundary.

### Gradient Masking Types

Gradient masking breaks gradient-based attacks by making gradients uninformative. Three subtypes exist, each arising from a different mechanism.

**Shattered gradients**: Non-differentiable operations between input and loss destroy the gradient signal entirely. Examples include rounding, discrete quantization, and thermometer encoding (converting each input value into a one-hot encoded bit vector). During forward pass, the non-differentiable operation runs normally. During backward pass, the gradient is zero or undefined because no smooth path exists through the operation. AutoGrad cannot differentiate through a discrete decision (like rounding 3.4 to 3). PGD and other gradient-based attacks fail because they cannot compute a meaningful direction to step.

*Why do non-differentiable operations break attacks?* PGD works by iteratively computing the loss gradient with respect to the input, then taking a step in the direction of maximum gradient (toward higher loss). If the gradient is undefined or zero, the algorithm has no direction to step. The perturbations become random noise rather than adaptive adversarial modifications. Standard automatic differentiation (reverse-mode accumulation in PyTorch or TensorFlow) returns zero for undefined gradients, halting the attack. The optimizer receives no signal about which direction to move, so each step is a random walk.

**Vanishing gradients**: A differentiable operation produces near-zero gradients carrying almost no information. Gradient clipping, bounding activations to a narrow range, or cascading many small-magnitude operations can create this effect. The gradient exists mathematically, but is numerically so small for gradient-based optimization to stall. PGD with a standard learning rate (like $2/255$ for $L_{\infty}$ attacks) produces imperceptible perturbations because each step advances by a tiny fraction of the available budget.

Consider a defense applying `bounded-ReLU`: $ReLU(x)$ clipped to $[0, 0.1]$. The gradient of such an activation is either $0\; (\text{if}\; x < 0)$ or $1\; (\text{if}\; 0 < x < 0.1)$, and effectively $0$ elsewhere. Cascading many such bounded activations causes gradients to vanish by repeated multiplication: if 10 layers each multiply by 0.1, the overall gradient is 0.1 raised to the 10th power, which is approximately `1e-10`. This is effectively zero in floating-point arithmetic. PGD cannot use vanishingly small gradients to make progress. The algorithm computes a step size of 0 and makes no advancement. A defense using this technique appears robust against PGD, but the robustness is an artifact of numerical instability rather than a fundamental defense property. This is why practitioners sometimes call vanishing gradients *"gradient starvation."* The network is not robustly trained. It simply provides no signal for optimization.

Vanishing gradients are particularly insidious because they can arise unintentionally. A poorly chosen activation function (e.g., tanh in deep networks), combined with certain weight initializations, can cause gradients to vanish during training. A researcher might implement a model with this property and observe improved robustness under PGD without realizing the improvement comes from vanishing gradients rather than learned robustness.

**Stochastic gradients**: Randomized computation makes gradients noisy and unreliable from step to step. Dropout is a common culprit: during training, researchers use dropout for regularization, and the system averages gradients over many stochastic forward-backward passes. But *what if the defense applies dropout at inference time?* Every forward pass produces slightly different outputs due to random mask drops. Gradients computed on one pass lack validity for the next pass, causing iterative attacks to converge toward false minima or plateau entirely. Random noise injection before classification (e.g., adding Gaussian noise and rounding) has the same effect. Each forward pass sees a different random input, making gradients unpredictable and optimization unstable. Examples include dropout at inference time, random noise injection before classification, or randomized thresholding. Computing a gradient on one run produces a different result on the next run at the same input.

Iterative gradient-based attacks require consistent gradient signals to build progress across steps. A perturbation computed to maximize loss at step 1 may no longer improve loss at step 2 because the model's stochastic computation changed. The defense's randomness acts as a moving target, preventing attacks from converging to a stable adversarial example.

*Why does randomization break attacks?* Iterative attacks like PGD assume the gradient signal is stable. If I step in the direction of highest gradient, I move toward higher loss. But if the gradient changes randomly between steps, there is no stable direction. The attacker cannot accumulate progress across steps. After 20 steps of random, contradictory gradient signals, the adversarial perturbation is essentially random noise rather than a carefully crafted attack. The defense's randomness decouples each attack step from the previous ones, preventing coordinated optimization. From the attacker's perspective, each iteration of PGD finds a gradient pointing in a seemingly useful direction, but the defense's randomness means this direction is invalid by the next step. The attack wastes its budget on steps failing to accumulate into a strong adversarial example.

One might ask: *why not attack deterministically (i.e., average the randomness away)?* Some adaptive attacks try this by running multiple forward-backward passes at each point and averaging gradients. But if the randomness is truly independent and uniform, averaging helps less than one might expect. A defense can generate diverse random seeds across forward passes to make gradient averaging harder.

Detection of masking uses three heuristics. First, evaluate the defense under random search: generate 10,000 random perturbations within the attack budget and report the fraction fooling the model. If random search finds many adversarial examples while PGD finds none, gradient masking is likely. Random search is optimization-free. It does not depend on gradients at all. If random search succeeds but PGD fails, gradients were the problem. Random search is a simple diagnostic: if adversarial examples exist in the attack budget, random sampling will eventually find them, regardless of gradient issues. Second, visualize the loss surface by computing gradients at multiple points nearby the input. If gradients are sparse, discontinuous, or near-zero, masking is present. A smooth loss surface has consistent gradient directions, whereas a masked defense has jagged, near-zero, or inconsistent gradients. Third, compute an empirical Lipschitz bound by sampling finite-difference gradients. If the bound is extremely loose or sampled gradients diverge far from analytical gradients, masking is present. A Lipschitz bound estimates how much the output can change for a small change in input. If the model is non-differentiable or has numerical instabilities (gradient masking), finite-difference estimates will diverge from automatic differentiation gradients, signaling trouble.

A fourth heuristic involves loss surface visualization. Compute the loss at many points in a grid around the input and plot the surface. A normal, non-masked network has a smooth, convex-like loss surface with a clear gradient direction. A masked defense has jagged, flat, or discontinuous terrain. Zero or near-zero gradients show up as flat plateaus. Discontinuities appear as sharp cliffs or holes in the surface.

### Backward Pass Differentiable Approximation (BPDA)

BPDA is an adaptive attack breaking shattered-gradient defenses. The core idea: in the backward pass only, substitute a differentiable approximation for the non-differentiable operation. The forward pass remains original.

Example: a defense applies thermometer encoding (non-differentiable bit operations) before classification. Standard PGD cannot differentiate through this. In BPDA, the forward pass runs the true thermometer encoding. The backward pass replaces the bit operation with the identity function (a differentiable approximation). Gradients flow through the approximation, giving PGD meaningful attack directions.

*How does this work mechanically?* Suppose the defense applies $f(x) = round(x * 256) / 256$ (quantization to 256 levels). The gradient of round is undefined almost everywhere. In BPDA, the forward pass computes $f(x)$ truthfully. During backpropagation, instead of asking for the gradient of round, we ask for the gradient of the identity function $g(x) = x$. The identity has gradient 1 everywhere, so gradients flow backward cleanly. The perturbation learned using $g(x)$ lacks perfect calibration for $f(x)$, but it approximates $f(x)$ well enough because the operations are monotonic. Input increases correspond to output increases in a predictable way, so the attack direction remains valid even if the magnitude differs.

The approximation must be close enough to the true operation for the gradient direction to remain valid. Identity works for thermometer encoding because the operation is monotonic in its input. For other operations, a smoother approximation is needed. For instance, to approximate a hard step function ($0 \;\text{if}~ x < 0,\; 1 ;\text{if}\; x >= 0$), using a sigmoid approximation $sigma(k*x)$ allows gradients to flow and guides the attack toward the decision boundary. The sigmoid is differentiable everywhere and smoothly approximates the step function's behavior. By using this smooth approximation during backpropagation, BPDA recovers a usable gradient signal.

BPDA is an adaptive attack requiring knowledge of the defense's architecture and parameters. The attacker must know exactly which operation is non-differentiable in order to replace it with an approximation in the backward pass. The attacker can identify the defense even if defenders attempt to hide or keep it proprietary. BPDA assumes white-box access or sufficient reverse-engineering to identify the non-differentiable component. If the attacker has access to the defense source code or can query the model to identify anomalies (zero gradients, discontinuous outputs), they can infer the non-differentiable operation.

*Why is BPDA an adaptive attack?* Because it requires explicit knowledge of the defense mechanism. A non-adaptive attacker using standard PGD does not know about the thermometer encoding. They apply standard gradient ascent and fail. An adaptive attacker, by contrast, audits the defense code, sees thermometer encoding, and specifically designs BPDA to circumvent it by approximating the gradient. The adaptive attacker tailors the attack to the defense's specific mechanism.

BPDA has limitations. Researchers must choose the approximation carefully. A poor approximation can mislead the attack in the wrong direction. Researchers typically iterate on the approximation function to find one working well empirically. For example, if identity doesn't work well for an operation, trying tanh or sigmoid approximations may recover attack success. The literature calls the backward pass approximation the *"obfuscated gradient trick"* because it works around the obfuscated (hidden) gradient.

> **Key insight:** BPDA works because most non-differentiable operations in defenses are monotonic or near-monotonic. Thermometer encoding, for example, always encodes larger input values to higher-index one-hot positions. Quantization is also monotonic. An approximation like identity captures the essential direction even if the magnitude differs. This works less well for non-monotonic operations (e.g., operations capable of mapping two nearby inputs to distant outputs), but most practical defenses use monotonic preprocessing.

### Falsely Secure Defenses Case Studies

**Thermometer encoding** (Buckman et al., 2018): Researchers proposed encoding each input value as a one-hot bit vector before passing it to the classifier. Programmers convert each pixel value x in $[0, 1]$ to a binary representation and then to a one-hot vector, increasing the number of channels dramatically. The claim was 83% accuracy under FGSM (one-step attack) and they argued the discretization prevented gradient-based attacks. Within months, Athalye, Carlini, and Wagner applied BPDA, dropping accuracy to 12% under adaptive PGD. Buckman et al. published the defense without testing against BPDA or any adaptive attack. Buckman et al. had evaluated only against FGSM, a known-weak attack from 2014.

*What went wrong?* The researchers did not apply Carlini's evaluation checklist. They chose only a weak, single-step attack. They did not check for gradient masking using random search. They did not test against adaptive attacks. Within weeks, researchers broke the defense. The lesson: choose a strong adaptive attack baseline (PGD-100 minimum), test against multiple attacks, check for masking heuristics, and provide code for independent verification.

**Defense-GAN** (Samangouei et al., 2018): Researchers trained a generative model to project adversarial inputs back toward the data manifold, then classification proceeded on the cleaned input. The idea was intuitive: use a GAN to *"restore"* adversarial examples to clean examples before the classifier sees them. Researchers tested only against FGSM and basic PGD, reporting 69% accuracy under attack. The defense confused the GAN's generation process with genuine robustness. Carlini and colleagues showed adversaries could fool the GAN itself. Adversarial examples could fool the generative model, or the attacker could provide a differentiable approximation of the GAN's output (bypassing the non-differentiable generation step). Accuracy under adaptive attack dropped to 23%. Again, evaluation against weak, non-adaptive attacks gave a false sense of security.

These cases follow a pattern: publish a defense, evaluate only against FGSM or basic PGD (weak single-step or 10-step attacks), claim high robustness. The following year, researchers apply adaptive attacks (PGD-100 or stronger), check for gradient masking, and break the defense. Madry et al.'s 2019 paper established PGD-100 as the standard adaptive baseline and formalized the threat model. Carlini's 2019 evaluation guidelines codified best practices: test against multiple algorithms, use long-horizon iterative attacks (PGD-100 or larger step budgets), check for gradient masking heuristics, and publish code for independent verification.

These failures reflected systematic issues, not random events. The researchers conducted reasonable due diligence for 2018 standards, but the standards were insufficient. As the field evolved, the evaluation bar rose. By 2019, top venues rejected any defense paper without adaptive attack evaluation and gradient masking checks. The lesson learned is: assume an adaptive adversary exists and design defenses and evaluations accordingly.

### Certified Robustness Motivation

Empirical robustness has a fundamental ceiling: no finite set of attacks can guarantee the absence of adversarial examples. An adversary with more resources, more creativity, or access to a future strong attack algorithm may find an example the attacks missed. Certified robustness breaks this ceiling by providing a formal proof.

A certified defense guarantees the following: for an input x, the model outputs a label y, and no adversarial example within radius r of x can change the prediction to any label other than y. This guarantee holds regardless of attack strategy, attack strength, or attacker creativity. The proof is independent of which attacks researchers tried during evaluation. Even if someone develops a new, stronger attack tomorrow, the certificate remains valid. The defense provides a mathematical guarantee, not a best-guess estimate. This is the core difference: empirical robustness is about what we have tested. Certified robustness is about what we can prove about the untested regions of the input space.

Why does certification require a different approach? Empirical evaluation answers a different question from certification. Empirical evaluation asks: *"Against the attacks I tested, how robust is the model?"* Certification asks: *"Against all possible attacks, what radius can I guarantee?"* Empirical evaluation is an upper bound on robustness (I failed to find an adversarial example with these attacks, but one may exist). Certification is a lower bound on robustness (I can prove no adversarial example exists within this radius). A certificate of robustness is fundamentally stronger than an empirical evaluation.

Two properties are central to certification. Soundness: if the defense issues a certificate (e.g., *"the guarantee holds within radius r"*), the guarantee never fails. Falsely issuing a certificate is a catastrophic failure. Certification only makes sense if it is sound. Unsound certificates are worse than no defense at all because they create a false sense of security. Completeness: the defense issues a certificate whenever one exists. A defense issuing certificates only for trivially easy examples (radius 0) is unsound but useless. The best certified defenses balance soundness (always correct) with completeness (covering most inputs). A complete but unsound defense is incorrect. A sound but incomplete defense is conservative but reliable. Incompleteness is often acceptable in practice. Issuing a certificate for 80% of inputs with high confidence is better than issuing unsound certificates for all inputs.

The distinction between empirical and certified robustness is profound. Empirical robustness depends on the specific attacks tested. If I test against 20 attacks and all fail to fool the model, I learn nothing about attacks 21-infinity. Certified robustness, by contrast, is universal: a proof holds against all possible attacks within the radius. Researchers sometimes call certified robustness *"provable"* robustness. Defenders can deploy a certified defense with confidence in safety-critical settings (e.g., autonomous vehicles, medical diagnosis) because the guarantee is mathematically sound.

From a practical perspective, empirical robustness evaluation will always play a role. Certified defenses currently sacrifice significant accuracy for certification (a model with certified robustness to 0.3 may drop from 95% clean accuracy to 85% or lower). Empirical evaluation helps identify which attacks and defenses are competitive and which ideas are worth pursuing. But for deployment in critical systems, empirical robustness is insufficient. Only certified robustness provides a guarantee.

A defense may be sound but incomplete (conservative, issuing certificates only when highly confident). This is preferable to unsound but complete. An unsound defense is worse than empirical evaluation because it creates a false sense of formal security when guarantees hold incorrectly. In practice, certified defenses sacrifice some accuracy for certification: a model with a certified guarantee for radius 0.3 may have lower accuracy on clean images than an empirically defended model. This tradeoff is intentional. The certified defense's value lies in its formal proof, not in maximizing accuracy.

Certified robustness is the foundation for the next two modules: randomized smoothing constructs a certified defense by adding Gaussian noise (`lesson 17`) and proves adversaries cannot fool a classifier trained on the smoothed model within a radius determined by the noise level. Formal verification methods (IBP, CROWN, SMT) provide certified bounds over the neural network's output by over-approximating all possible values the network can produce for inputs in a ball around x (`lesson 18`). Neither approach is perfect (they sacrifice accuracy for certification), but practitioners prove both sound.

## Summary

Empirical robustness is only as strong as the attacks used to evaluate it. An insufficient attack suite creates a false sense of security. Gradient masking (shattered, vanishing, or stochastic gradients) breaks gradient-based attacks, but BPDA and random search expose masked defenses. Historically, attackers broke defenses claiming high robustness against weak attacks (FGSM) within months using stronger attacks. Certified robustness escapes this arms race by providing a formal proof independent of attack strategy.
